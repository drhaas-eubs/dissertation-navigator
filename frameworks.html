Frameworks can chain — but don't stack for the sake of it. One coherent example, for an external-to-internal-to-adoption study, runs: PESTEL → Porter's Five Forces → RBV/VRIN → Dynamic Capabilities → TAM/UTAUT (external environment → industry → internal resources → transformation capacity → user adoption). It's a useful illustration of how lenses connect.

The caution: this is an example, not a template to apply wholesale. Bolting five frameworks onto one dissertation usually produces descriptive, checklist analysis and reads to examiners as a weak, unfocused methodology. Most strong dissertations are driven by one or two well-justified frameworks that genuinely fit the research question. Choose for fit, then go deep — not wide.

Strategy · Competitive · Michael E. Porter

Five Forces

Industry attractiveness is set by five structural forces, not by competitors alone.
  • Rivalry among existing competitors sits at the centre
  • Threat of new entrants and of substitutes bound it
  • Bargaining power of buyers and of suppliers flank it
  • Use when the question concerns industry structure or profitability
REFERENCEPorter, M.E. (1980) Competitive Strategy: Techniques for Analyzing Industries and Competitors. New York: Free Press.

Strategy · Competitive · Michael E. Porter

Value Chain

Competitive advantage is found by disaggregating the firm into value-creating activities.
  • Primary activities: inbound logistics → operations → outbound → marketing → service
  • Support activities: firm infrastructure, HRM, technology, procurement
  • Margin is what remains after the cost of performing activities
  • Use to locate where value and cost are actually created
REFERENCEPorter, M.E. (1985) Competitive Advantage: Creating and Sustaining Superior Performance. New York: Free Press.

Strategy · Competitive · Jay B. Barney

Resource-Based View (VRIN)

Sustained advantage comes from resources that are Valuable, Rare, Inimitable and Non-substitutable.
  • Advantage lives inside the firm, not only in industry position
  • A resource must clear all four VRIN tests to sustain advantage
  • Complements Porter's outside-in view with an inside-out one
  • Use when the answer lies in the firm's assets or capabilities
REFERENCEBarney, J. (1991) 'Firm resources and sustained competitive advantage', Journal of Management, 17(1), pp. 99-120.

Strategy · Competitive · Teece, Pisano & Shuen

Dynamic Capabilities

In fast-moving markets, advantage is the capacity to sense, seize and reconfigure.
  • Sensing: identify opportunities and threats
  • Seizing: mobilise resources to capture value
  • Reconfiguring/transforming: renew the asset base
  • Use for questions about adaptation, agility or transformation
REFERENCETeece, D.J., Pisano, G. and Shuen, A. (1997) 'Dynamic capabilities and strategic management', Strategic Management Journal, 18(7), pp. 509-533.

Strategy · Competitive · Clayton M. Christensen

Disruptive Innovation

Entrants win from below, not by out-performing incumbents at their own game.
  • Incumbents over-serve mainstream customers with sustaining innovation
  • Disruptors enter at the low end or in new markets
  • They improve until they meet mainstream needs, then displace
  • Use to explain why strong firms lose to simpler offerings
REFERENCEChristensen, C.M. (1997) The Innovator's Dilemma. Boston: Harvard Business School Press.

Strategy · Competitive · Osterwalder & Pigneur

Business Model Canvas

A business model is nine linked building blocks on a single shared canvas.
  • Value proposition sits at the centre
  • Right side faces the market: customers, channels, relationships, revenue
  • Left side is the engine: activities, resources, partners, costs
  • Use to map or redesign how a business creates and captures value
REFERENCEOsterwalder, A. and Pigneur, Y. (2010) Business Model Generation. Hoboken: Wiley.

Technology Adoption · Tornatzky & Fleischer

TOE Framework

Adoption is shaped by three contexts: Technological, Organisational, Environmental.
  • Technological: the innovation's characteristics and fit
  • Organisational: size, structure, slack, managerial support
  • Environmental: industry, competitors, regulation
  • Use for firm-level (not individual) adoption questions
REFERENCETornatzky, L.G. and Fleischer, M. (1990) The Processes of Technological Innovation. Lexington: Lexington Books.

Technology Adoption · Fred D. Davis

Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)

Use intention is driven by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use.
  • Perceived usefulness → attitude and intention
  • Perceived ease of use → usefulness and attitude
  • Intention → actual system use
  • Use for individual-level acceptance of a technology
REFERENCEDavis, F.D. (1989) 'Perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and user acceptance of information technology', MIS Quarterly, 13(3), pp. 319-340.

Technology Adoption · Venkatesh et al.

UTAUT

Four determinants predict acceptance, moderated by age, gender and experience.
  • Performance expectancy and effort expectancy
  • Social influence and facilitating conditions
  • Moderators: age, gender, experience, voluntariness
  • Use for a unified, more complete acceptance model than TAM
REFERENCEVenkatesh, V., Morris, M.G., Davis, G.B. and Davis, F.D. (2003) 'User acceptance of information technology: toward a unified view', MIS Quarterly, 27(3), pp. 425-478.

Technology Adoption · Everett M. Rogers

Diffusion of Innovations

Innovations spread through a population in a predictable S-curve of adopter types.
  • Innovators and early adopters open the market
  • Early and late majority form the bulk
  • Laggards adopt last; a chasm can stall the curve
  • Use to explain the rate and pattern of adoption over time
REFERENCERogers, E.M. (2003) Diffusion of Innovations. 5th edn. New York: Free Press.

Change & Transformation · John P. Kotter

8-Step Change Model

Durable change follows eight sequential steps, beginning with urgency.
  • Create urgency, build a guiding coalition, form a vision
  • Communicate it, empower action, generate short-term wins
  • Consolidate gains, then anchor change in the culture
  • Use to structure or diagnose an organisational change effort
REFERENCEKotter, J.P. (1996) Leading Change. Boston: Harvard Business School Press.

Change & Transformation · David L. Rogers

Digital Transformation Playbook

Digital change is a strategy shift across five domains, not an IT upgrade.
  • Customers, competition, data, innovation, value
  • Networks replace mass markets; platforms replace pipelines
  • Value proposition must be continually adapted
  • Use to frame enterprise-level digital strategy
REFERENCERogers, D.L. (2016) The Digital Transformation Playbook. New York: Columbia Business School Publishing.

Change & Transformation · N. Venkatraman

IT-Enabled Transformation (Five Levels)

IT value rises from localised automation up to business-scope redefinition.
  • Level 1-2 (evolutionary): localised exploitation, internal integration
  • Level 3-5 (revolutionary): process redesign, network redesign, scope redefinition
  • Higher levels need deeper organisational change for higher return
  • Use to position the ambition of an IT-enabled initiative
REFERENCEVenkatraman, N. (1994) 'IT-enabled business transformation: from automation to business scope redefinition', Sloan Management Review, 35(2), pp. 73-87.

Design Thinking

Innovation begins with human desirability, balanced against feasibility and viability.
  • Inspiration, ideation, implementation as overlapping spaces
  • Empathise → define → ideate → prototype → test
  • Desirability × feasibility × viability must all hold
  • Use for user-centred innovation and problem framing
REFERENCEBrown, T. (2009) Change by Design. New York: HarperBusiness.

Innovation & Entrepreneurship · Saras D. Sarasvathy

Effectuation

Expert entrepreneurs start from means and control the future rather than predict it.
  • Bird-in-hand: start with who you are, know, and know of
  • Affordable loss, not expected return, bounds commitment
  • Crazy-quilt of partners; leverage surprise (lemonade)
  • Use to explain venture creation under true uncertainty
REFERENCESarasvathy, S.D. (2001) 'Causation and effectuation', Academy of Management Review, 26(2), pp. 243-263.

Innovation & Entrepreneurship · Blank & Dorf

TAM · SAM · SOM

Market size is three nested circles, from total demand down to what you can win.
  • TAM: total addressable market — all demand for the category
  • SAM: serviceable available market — the segment you can reach
  • SOM: serviceable obtainable market — what you can realistically capture
  • Use to size a market credibly and avoid top-down hand-waving
REFERENCEBlank, S. and Dorf, B. (2012) The Startup Owner's Manual. Pescadero: K&S Ranch.

PESTEL

The macro-environment scanned across six external forces.
  • Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal

  • Feeds naturally into industry (Five Forces) analysis

Environmental Scan · Learned, Christensen, Andrews & Guth

SWOT

A 2×2 that pairs internal strengths and weaknesses with external opportunities and threats.
  • Internal: strengths and weaknesses
  • External: opportunities and threats
  • Value comes from the cross-pairs (S-O, W-T), not the lists
  • Use as a summarising bridge, backed by deeper analysis
REFERENCELearned, E.P., Christensen, C.R., Andrews, K.R. and Guth, W.D. (1969) Business Policy: Text and Cases. Homewood: Irwin.

Balanced Scorecard

Performance viewed through four linked perspectives, not finance alone.
  • Financial, Customer, Internal Process, Learning & Growth
  • Links strategy to measurable objectives in each
  • Learning drives process, which drives customer, which drives financial
  • Use to translate strategy into a measurement system
REFERENCEKaplan, R.S. and Norton, D.P. (1996) The Balanced Scorecard. Boston: Harvard Business School Press.

Research Design · Robert K. Yin

Case Study Research

An empirical inquiry into a phenomenon in its real-world context, using multiple evidence.
  • Bound the case; justify single vs multiple
  • Use several sources of evidence and triangulate
  • Build a case-study database and chain of evidence
  • Use for how/why questions in a live context
REFERENCEYin, R.K. (2018) Case Study Research and Applications. 6th edn. Thousand Oaks: SAGE.

Research Design · Kathleen M. Eisenhardt

Building Theory from Cases

Theory can be built inductively from the patterns across a set of cases.
  • Select cases for theoretical, not statistical, reasons
  • Analyse within-case, then search cross-case patterns
  • Iterate between data and emerging theory to sharpen constructs
  • Use to generate testable theory from rich case data
REFERENCEEisenhardt, K.M. (1989) 'Building theories from case study research', Academy of Management Review, 14(4), pp. 532-550.

Research Design · Creswell & Plano Clark

Mixed Methods

Combine quantitative and qualitative strands with an explicit integration rationale.
  • Convergent, explanatory-sequential or exploratory-sequential
  • State the sequence, priority and point of integration
  • Integrate at interpretation — don't staple two studies together
  • Use when neither strand alone answers the question
REFERENCECreswell, J.W. and Plano Clark, V.L. (2018) Designing and Conducting Mixed Methods Research. 3rd edn. Thousand Oaks: SAGE.

Six recursive phases to build patterns of meaning from qualitative data.

  • A theme is a pattern of meaning, not a frequent word
  • State inductive vs theoretical; keep a codebook

Data Analysis · Glaser & Strauss

Grounded Theory

Theory emerges from data through constant comparison until saturation.
  • Open, axial and selective coding
  • Constant comparison between incidents and categories
  • Theoretical sampling until no new properties emerge
  • Use to build theory where little exists
REFERENCEGlaser, B.G. and Strauss, A.L. (1967) The Discovery of Grounded Theory. Chicago: Aldine.

Qualitative Data Analysis

Analysis is three concurrent flows: data condensation, display, and conclusion-drawing.
  • Condensation: select, focus, simplify the data
  • Display: matrices and networks make patterns visible
  • Conclusion drawing and verification run throughout
  • Use for a structured, auditable qualitative workflow
REFERENCEMiles, M.B., Huberman, A.M. and Saldaña, J. (2020) Qualitative Data Analysis: A Methods Sourcebook. 4th edn. Thousand Oaks: SAGE.

Deductive, Inductive & Abductive Reasoning

The logical direction between theory and data.
  • Deduction → theory to data (test)
  • Induction → data to theory (build)
  • Abduction → best explanation, refined iteratively
  • Let your question's verb guide the choice

Sampling Strategies

Who or what you study — probability vs non-probability.
  • Probability → random, stratified; enables generalisation
  • Non-probability → purposive, snowball; qualitative depth
  • Size for power (quant) or saturation (qual)
  • Own the selection bias in your limitations

Literature Review — Synthesis, Not Summary

Synthesis, not summary — build an argument.
  • Summary (weak) → source-by-source description
  • Synthesis (strong) → organised by theme, evaluated
  • Funnel: field → debate → your gap
  • End by stating the gap your question fills

  • Confidentiality, anonymity, secure storage (GDPR)
  • Approval BEFORE recruitment — sequence matters
  • Debrief protocol required if deception is used
